South Texas Flooding: The Difference Between a Watch and a Warning

NOVEMBER 8, 2026 · TNS NEWS DESK

The most dangerous assumption during a wet weekend is that every county faces the same threat.

South Texas entered Sunday with a legitimate heavy-rain concern. NOAA’s Weather Prediction Center described repeated thunderstorms and possible flash flooding, especially where rainfall overlaps cities, steep terrain or other flood-prone locations. Its early-morning discussion called for locally intense rainfall, including amounts exceeding four inches in parts of the South Texas Plains.

But there is an important distinction between a forecast discussion, a flood watch and a flash flood warning. A discussion explains the meteorological setup. A watch means conditions favor flooding over an area and time window. A warning means flooding is occurring or imminent in the specified location. They are not interchangeable, and the status can change as new observations arrive.

The danger is often concentrated. One neighborhood may receive a steady, manageable rain while another sees thunderstorms repeatedly track overhead. Low-water crossings, frontage roads and poorly drained intersections can become hazardous before nearby residents realize conditions have changed.

The story today is therefore about timing and geography, not a blanket declaration that all of South Texas is underwater. Anyone traveling should check the NWS warning map for their exact route. A forecast for a county is not permission to drive through moving water.

The next several hours will reveal where the heaviest bands actually set up. That is what TNS will watch—not merely the most alarming possible rainfall number.
